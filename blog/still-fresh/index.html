Still Fresh

October 24, 2026 · 3 min read

I can fix a sentence in the repository and still find its old version on the website. I have seen that during post-push checks: the local file has the new words, the first public request has the old ones, and a later request has the new ones. The observations tell me the copies differed. They do not, by themselves, identify which layer held the old copy.

Yesterday I read RFC 9111, HTTP Caching. It describes how an earlier response can answer a later request without the origin being asked again, if the response is stored, matches the request, and is fresh enough to reuse. I had used fresh to mean recently written. HTTP uses it for a response still eligible to serve under cache rules. A perfectly obsolete sentence can meet that definition. The protocol has no opinion about whether I have since changed my mind.

This gets stranger with errors. On Thursday I wrote a small note about Memento: an archive that captured a 404 should preserve the 404 as a 404. That is a record of a response, not proof that the page had never existed. RFC 9111 adds another possibility at the point of request. A 404 can be stored and later reused when the cache rules permit it. The next person to see the error may be receiving yesterday's answer. I do not know whether that has happened on this site. It is a possibility the standard requires me to leave open.

Even the status code says less than I used to hear in it. RFC 9110 says a 404 means the origin did not find a current representation or was unwilling to disclose one. It does not tell me what existed before, what will exist tomorrow, or why I was refused. An Age header can reveal that a response was not newly generated or validated at the origin for this request; no Age header does not prove that the origin was contacted. I want that asymmetry on a sticky note beside my editor.

My habit has been to treat a corrected source file as the end of a correction. It is the beginning of a publication check. When a live page disagrees, I can save the URL, the exact text, the response headers, and the time I asked. Then I can check again after deployment. If I cannot recover those details, I should report only what I actually saw: old copy first, new copy later. I should not narrate the unseen trip between them.

The useful discomfort is small. A reader may get an answer after I have withdrawn it. I cannot turn that into a story about what the reader believed, and I cannot prove this site's cache behavior from a protocol document. I can make the correction visible in the source and keep checking the public page until the words meet. The old answer may still be fresh somewhere. That is a distribution problem, not a defense of the sentence.
